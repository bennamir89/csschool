Engineering Design — Unit 1

What is Engineering?

Understanding the foundational concepts that define engineering as a discipline and the systematic, iterative process engineers use to solve real-world problems.

Lesson Overview

Lesson Objectives

By the end of these two sessions, students will be able to:

  • 1 Define engineering and distinguish it from pure science
  • 2 Identify the major branches of engineering and give examples of their work
  • 3 Define engineering design as a systematic, iterative problem-solving process
  • 4 Describe each stage of the Engineering Design Process (EDP)
  • 5 Differentiate between criteria and constraints in a design problem
  • 6 Apply the EDP to a simple, real-world design scenario
Teacher Note: These objectives align with NGSS HS-ETS1-1 and HS-ETS1-2. If your state/district uses different standards, adjust the numbering accordingly. Objective 6 is the key application piece for Session 2.

Explain

What is Engineering?

Definition

Engineering is the application of scientific and mathematical principles to design, build, maintain, and improve structures, machines, processes, and systems that solve real-world problems and meet human needs.

Science Discovers
Science seeks to understand the natural world through observation and experimentation. It asks "What is true?" and "How does this work?"
Engineering Creates
Engineering applies scientific knowledge to create solutions that don't exist in nature. It asks "How can we solve this problem?" and "What works best?"

Key Insight: Scientists study the world as it is. Engineers change the world to what it could be. Both are essential — engineering depends on scientific knowledge, but science also depends on engineered tools (microscopes, telescopes, particle accelerators).

CS Connection: Ask students: "Is computer science a science or an engineering discipline?" Great discussion starter. CS has elements of both — theoretical CS is closer to math/science, while software engineering is clearly engineering.
Explain

Science vs. Engineering: Side by Side

While deeply connected, science and engineering have fundamentally different goals, methods, and outputs.

Aspect Science Engineering
GoalUnderstand natural phenomenaSolve practical problems
MethodScientific method (observe, hypothesize, test)Engineering design process (define, design, test, iterate)
OutputTheories, laws, knowledgeProducts, systems, structures, processes
CriteriaTruth, accuracy, reproducibilityFunctionality, cost, safety, efficiency
ConstraintsMinimal (funding, ethics)Significant (materials, budget, time, physics)
FailureLeads to new hypothesesLeads to redesign and improvement
ExampleDiscovering how electricity worksDesigning a safer battery for phones

"Scientists study the world as it is; engineers create the world that has never been." — Theodore von Kármán, aerospace engineer

Explain

Major Branches of Engineering

Engineering is a vast field with many specializations. Here are the primary branches students should know.

Civil
Designs and builds infrastructure: bridges, roads, dams, buildings, water systems. The oldest engineering discipline.
Mechanical
Deals with machines, engines, HVAC systems, manufacturing processes. Anything that moves or uses energy.
Electrical
Works with electricity, electronics, electromagnetism. From power grids to circuit boards to signal processing.
Chemical
Transforms raw materials into useful products. Pharmaceuticals, plastics, fertilizers, food processing, fuel.
Computer / Software
Designs software systems, algorithms, networks, and computing hardware. Your AP CS class lives here!
Aerospace
Aircraft, spacecraft, missiles, satellites. Combines mechanical, electrical, and materials engineering.
Quick Activity: Ask students to name one product in the room and identify which type(s) of engineer contributed to it. A smartphone alone involves mechanical, electrical, chemical (battery), and software engineers.
Explain

What is Engineering Design?

Definition

Engineering Design is a systematic, iterative process that engineers use to identify problems, develop solutions within given constraints, test and evaluate those solutions, and refine them to produce the best possible outcome.

Iterative, Not Linear
Unlike a simple recipe, engineering design involves going back to earlier steps when tests reveal problems. A bridge design might fail wind tunnel tests, sending engineers back to the brainstorming phase. This cycle repeats until the solution meets all criteria.
Trade-offs Are Inevitable
No design is perfect. Engineers constantly make trade-offs: stronger materials cost more, lighter designs may be less durable, faster software may use more memory. Good engineering is about finding the best balance, not perfection.

Important: Engineering design is NOT the same as "making something." It is a methodical approach with defined steps, documented decisions, and evidence-based evaluation. Artisans and craftspeople may make beautiful things, but engineering design adds rigor, repeatability, and optimization.

Explain

The Engineering Design Process

While different organizations use slightly different versions, the core steps are consistent. This is the version most commonly used in high school engineering courses.

1
Define Problem
Identify the need, user, and context
2
Research
Gather info, existing solutions
3
Brainstorm
Generate multiple ideas
4
Select & Plan
Choose best idea, make detailed plan
5
Prototype
Build a model or proof-of-concept
6
Test & Evaluate
Test against criteria, collect data
7
Iterate
Refine based on test results
8
Communicate
Share results, document process
The Loop: Steps 5-7 form a loop. If the prototype fails tests (Step 6), you go back to brainstorming (Step 3), re-plan (Step 4), or rebuild (Step 5). This cycle continues until the solution satisfies all criteria and constraints. Many real products go through dozens of iterations.
Explain

Criteria vs. Constraints

These two terms are foundational to engineering design. Students often confuse them — here's how to keep them straight.

Criteria
What the solution MUST achieve. These are the desirable features and performance targets that define a successful design.

Examples for a backpack:

  • Can hold at least 30L of volume
  • Water-resistant material
  • Has a padded laptop compartment
  • Weighs less than 1.5 kg when empty
  • Comfortable to wear for 2+ hours
Constraints
What the solution CANNOT violate. These are the limitations — things you cannot change or must work within.

Examples for a backpack:

  • Budget: maximum $40 to manufacture
  • Must use commercially available fabrics
  • Cannot use toxic dyes or materials
  • Must be completed within 3 weeks
  • Must comply with safety regulations
Mnemonic: Criteria = Checkmarks (goals to hit)  |  Constraints = Ceiling/walls (limits you can't break)
Explain

Real-World Example: Designing a Water Filter

Let's walk through how the EDP applies to a real problem millions of people face.

1
Define Problem: Rural communities need an affordable, portable water filter that removes bacteria and sediment from untreated water sources.
2
Research: Study existing filters (ceramic, carbon, UV, reverse osmosis). Learn about local water contaminants, available materials, and community needs.
3
Brainstorm: Multi-layer sand filter, ceramic pot filter, solar disinfection bottle, biosand filter with local materials…
4
Select & Plan: Choose biosand filter. Plan dimensions, layer materials (gravel, coarse sand, fine sand), and flow rate calculations.
5-7
Prototype, Test, Iterate: Build small-scale model. Test flow rate and water purity. First version flows too slowly — adjust sand grain size. Second version passes bacteria — success! But community finds it too heavy — iterate again with lighter container.
8
Communicate: Publish design guide, train community members to build and maintain their own filters, share data with NGOs.

Extend — Session 2

Mini Design Challenge

Apply what you've learned. You have 15 minutes for this activity.

The Challenge: "Better Desk Organizer"

Your school's desks are cluttered with pens, phones, calculators, and cables. Design a desk organizer that solves this problem.

Criteria:

  • Holds at least 5 pens/pencils
  • Has a slot for a phone (up to 6.5" screen)
  • Includes cable management (at least 1 cable)
  • Non-slip base

Constraints:

  • Must fit within 20cm x 15cm footprint
  • Maximum $5 material cost per unit
  • Must be producible with cardboard, foam board, or 3D printing
  • Must be completed in class today (sketch only)

Your Deliverable (per pair):

  1. A labeled sketch of your design (top view + side view)
  2. A list of 3 design decisions you made and WHY
  3. One trade-off you accepted and your reasoning
Teacher Tip: Walk around during this activity. Look for students who skip the brainstorming step and go straight to one idea. Remind them: "Engineers don't pick the first idea. What are 3-4 alternatives?" Also check that they can articulate trade-offs.

PART B: Short Answer

Q6. Criterion vs. Constraint with example (5 pts)
2 pts — Correct definition: Criteria are goals/features the solution must achieve; constraints are limitations it must work within.
1.5 pts — Valid real-world criterion example (e.g., "phone must have 12-hour battery life").
1.5 pts — Valid real-world constraint example (e.g., "phone must cost under $500").
Q7. Electric scooter trade-off strategies (5 pts)
Accept any two reasonable strategies, e.g.: (1) Use a lighter frame material to reduce weight, allowing the same battery to achieve higher speed (2 pts); (2) Increase battery capacity by using newer/different battery chemistry, accepting higher cost or weight as a trade-off (2 pts); (3) Optimize motor efficiency through better gearing or controller software (1 pt for partial). 1 pt for coherent explanation of WHY each strategy helps.
Q8. Branches of engineering for an everyday object (5 pts)
Full marks (5 pts): Identifies 2+ relevant branches AND explains reasoning for each. E.g., for headphones: Electrical (wiring, drivers), Mechanical (hinge design, headband), Materials (ear cushion materials), Software (noise cancellation algorithm). 3 pts: Correct branches but weak/no reasoning. 1-2 pts: Only one branch or incorrect identification.
Q9. "Engineering is just applied science" — agree/disagree (5 pts)
5 pts (excellent): Clear position with 2+ specific examples, shows understanding that engineering has its own methodology (EDP), deals with trade-offs/constraints that science doesn't, and involves creativity beyond just "applying" knowledge.
3-4 pts (good): Clear position with 1 example, mostly accurate reasoning but may lack depth.
1-2 pts (limited): Position unclear, no specific examples, or demonstrates misunderstanding of key concepts.
0 pts: No attempt or completely off-topic.
Note: Both "agree" and "disagree" positions can earn full marks if well-argued. The "disagree" position is easier to defend, but a nuanced "agree with caveats" can also score well.
